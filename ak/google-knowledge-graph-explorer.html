A name, a number, and a bit of curiosity

I've been interested in how entities are represented across the digital space for quite a while. People, organisations, places, fictional characters; all the names we write into search boxes, and the rather less readable identifiers behind some of them. Eventually, looking through other people's tools became a good enough reason to try making my own.

Part of it was wanting something I enjoyed using. Most of the Knowledge Graph tools I had come across were made by SEOs, and interface design isn't exactly what we're famous for. Part of it was the familiar question: can I build this? Apparently, a perfectly adequate reason to spend considerably more time with API documentation than originally intended.

The explorer above searches records returned by Google Enterprise Knowledge Graph Basic. You can inspect identifiers and source references, compare entities, and look at the same record in different languages. I wanted the information to be easy to examine, especially when several results look suspiciously similar.

Google supplies the entity records; I built the interface around them. This is an independent project, and the available API fields are only a limited view of the information involved in Google's search systems. Still, there's quite a lot to get curious about within that view.

Using the explorer

Start with a name, keyword or identifier. The example cards run searches too; they're starting points, rather than handpicked sets of results. Advanced search lets you choose a language, narrow the entity type and request up to 50 results. Google can return fewer. When you supply a recognised identifier, the tool performs a direct lookup, with the selected language; the type and result-count filters don't apply to that lookup.

  1. Search, then inspect. Open a result to see its available names, descriptions, types, identifiers, associated website and source references. A blank field remains a blank field; the tool doesn't fill it with a plausible guess.
  2. Compare records. Add up to three entities to the comparison tray, including results from different searches, then choose Compare. Shared values, differences and missing information are shown beside one another.
  3. Compare languages. Inside the inspector, select two languages to request the same identifier in each. This keeps the record fixed while changing the requested language.
  4. Follow the evidence. Visit the supplied sources, open Google Search, or investigate a Wikidata candidate through the separate disclosure.
  5. Keep what you need. Copy an identifier or search link, or export the entity comparison as CSV.

Read the labels for what they measure

API order preserves Google's returned order. The name-match badges describe text similarity to your query. An exact match means the normalised text matches; it doesn't mean the result is the person or organisation you intended. Normalisation handles details such as capitalisation, punctuation and spacing. It doesn't translate names or establish identity.

There is no entity-strength score here. A result appearing first, having a photograph, or containing a longer description doesn't give us a measurement of its authority or SEO value. Those would be rather large conclusions to extract from a small response.

Source and retrieval information help you keep track of what you're examining. Retrieved records when the tool processed the response, rather than when Google last updated the entity.

From strings to things

A name is a label. An entity is the particular person, place, organisation, work or other thing that label refers to in context. A knowledge graph represents entities and connections between them. Attributes describe them; relationships connect them. The distinction becomes useful as soon as the same word can mean several things, which happens almost immediately.

For this website, a simple relationship is that this page has an author, me, Alexander Kyriakou. The page and its author are separate entities. A name alone leaves some interpretation to the reader; an explicit relationship to an identified person makes the intended meaning clearer. We can apply the same approach to an organisation and its founder, a book and its author, or a film and its director.

Google introduced its Knowledge Graph on 16 May 2012, under the memorable phrase “things, not strings”. Its announcement used Taj Mahal to illustrate the ambiguity: a monument, a musician and other entities can share those words. Identifying the intended subject makes it possible to select relevant facts and help a searcher explore related subjects. There was a bigger ambition behind this: moving from finding information towards recognising what that information describes, and how it connects to other things.

Knowledge representation didn't begin with that announcement. Google's launch made a particular application of it highly visible. Freebase was an important earlier component: Google acquired Metaweb, the company behind it, in 2010. The later paper From Freebase to Wikidata: The Great Migration describes work to transfer Freebase content into Wikidata, including the difficulties of mapping different structures and community requirements. This wasn't simply a database changing its name.

That history helps explain why Freebase identifiers still turn up in present-day investigations. It also helps keep three things separate: knowledge graphs as a general approach, Google's Knowledge Graph, and open projects such as Wikidata. They have connections, but they aren't interchangeable databases.

What kind of entity are we looking at?

An entity's type describes what sort of thing it is. Thing is Schema.org's broadest type, which might feel like the machine has put remarkably little effort into the answer. More specific types supply the useful detail: a person, an organisation, a film, a place.

Types have broader parent types and can overlap. A Corporation is a kind of Organization; LocalBusiness sits under both Organization and Place in the Schema.org hierarchy. A response can list several types for one entity. Google's API example, for instance, includes both Thing and Person. Multiple labels don't automatically mean the source is confused.

The table covers the 22 type presets available in this explorer. Each name links to its Schema.org definition. The distinctions are examples to help you interpret a record, rather than claims about how Google currently classifies a particular person, business or work. This is a guide to the filter choices, not a complete inventory of Google's internal classifications or the Schema.org vocabulary.

All entities removes the type filter; it isn't another entity type. The presets narrow a keyword search, but they don't guarantee results or complete coverage. If a filter returns nothing, try the same query without it and inspect the types actually supplied. These labels describe the returned records, not their eligibility for a Knowledge Panel or a Google rich result.

Making sense of the identifiers

A machine ID, or MID, gives a record an identifier that doesn't depend on how its name happens to be written. Google's MID documentation distinguishes existing Google Knowledge Graph identifiers from the newer Cloud format. The explorer keeps them separate too.

Identifiers you may encounter
IdentifierWhere it belongsHow to read it
Google MID: /m/…Google / Freebase heritageA Google identifier in the Freebase-style namespace.
Google MID: /g/…Google Knowledge GraphAnother Google identifier namespace accepted by the explorer.
Cloud MID: c-…Cloud Knowledge GraphThe primary identifier used in the returned Cloud record; a Google MID may also be supplied.
Wikidata Q-ID: Q…WikidataIdentifies an item in a separate knowledge base.
Website @idYour published linked dataAn identifier you use for a node, often a URL under your own domain.

Cloud lookups can accept existing /m/… and /g/… identifiers while returning a Cloud MID as the primary ID and referencing the Google MID separately. Two different strings can therefore identify corresponding records in different namespaces. Keep the namespace with the value when recording your findings.

You may also encounter MREID, meaning machine-readable entity ID, in SEO articles. UpBuild's article on entity IDs is an interesting exploration of identifying entities and connecting them to website markup. Its own qualification deserves to travel with the advice: the proposed SEO benefits were unproven. Identifiers are useful without needing to assign them an imaginary ranking bonus.

A unique identifier can still be attached to the wrong thing

There are two separate jobs here: distinguishing records and deciding what those records represent. Unique IDs help with the first. The second still requires evidence. A website can link to the wrong Wikidata item; a source can contain an incorrect statement; two records can describe the same person. Giving those records tidy identifiers doesn't resolve the underlying disagreement.

When keeping your own research notes, record the identifier, source, requested language, retrieval time and the particular observation. “This record returned this website” is a much more useful note than “Google knows who this is”. It is also something you can check again.

Same name, different identity

A search for Alexander Kyriakou returns four entity records at the time of writing. Apparently, one of me wasn't enough. That doesn't tell us whether they describe different people, overlapping records about the same person, or a mixture of both. The shared name gives us somewhere to start.

This is where comparing fields becomes useful. Start with the identifiers, then examine descriptions, types, associated websites and source pages. If two records point to the same website, follow the link and check what the page actually describes. A personal homepage identifies its subject more clearly than an organisation's general staff directory.

Questions to keep open when comparing similar records
Possible explanationWhat to investigate
Different people share a nameBiographies, work, locations, official profiles and independently supported details that distinguish them.
Several records describe one personWhether the references consistently converge on the same identity, including matching external identifiers.
The records describe related thingsWhether one represents a person and another a business, brand, work or organisation associated with them.
There isn't enough informationWhich sources or fields would resolve the uncertainty. Leave the match undecided until then.

A business named after its founder is a useful example. The person's name can appear on the business website, and both may share a public address or photograph. That doesn't make the person and the business the same entity. Likewise, a book, its film adaptation and a character in the story can share a name while requiring separate records.

Check time as well. Different employers can reflect a career change; a different domain can reflect a move. A missing value supplies no opposing claim at all. Before calling two fields contradictory, make sure they're describing the same attribute, at the same level of detail, for the same period.

Use Wikidata as another source to examine

The optional Wikidata lookup begins with a candidate you choose. Search Wikidata, inspect the item, then enter its Q-ID in the explorer. The candidate stays separate from Google's record. A matching name is a reason to investigate it, not permission to merge its details into the Google result.

Wikidata's statements can include qualifiers, references and ranks. Those details matter when investigating an identity, and the explorer only retrieves selected fields. Open the original item when you need the surrounding evidence.

A few connections make the difference

Take Coraline, as a completely unbiased example. One title, a book, a film, and a fairly good demonstration of why names alone don't get us very far. The book was published in 2002; the film followed in 2009. They are related works, with different types and different people involved.

Here is a small map of those relationships. Each box represents an entity; each arrow makes a particular claim about its connection to another. Follow the arrow from the subject to the object: the film is based on the book. That direction is part of the meaning.

The useful part is the specificity. Neil Gaiman wrote the book; Henry Selick directed the film. “Connected to Coraline” loses that distinction. In a graph, the relationship matters as much as the two things it connects.

It also gives us a practical check for structured data. Linking the film to the book with isBasedOn describes an adaptation. Linking them with sameAs would claim that they are the same entity. A familiar title doesn't make that claim correct. And knowing these connections ourselves doesn't tell us which ones Google stores internally, exposes through an API, or uses for a particular search.

The same entity in another language

Athens and Αθήνα can refer to the same city. The label changes with the language; the subject doesn't. Wikidata makes this distinction particularly visible: an item has a Q-ID, with labels, descriptions and aliases maintained in different languages.

For Google, there is also a useful historical example in its 2013 explanation of photo search. The system used Freebase entities as image-class labels, distinguishing subjects through identifiers independent of their language-specific names. Bill Slawski explored this in his article on entity numbers in image search and Trends. These ideas have been around considerably longer than the current excitement about AI search.

For a useful test in this explorer, inspect one record and compare its identifier across two languages. Searching two translated names separately asks which candidates match each query. Looking up one identifier twice asks what the source returns for that same record under different language requests. Those are different experiments.

The tool offers 39 languages, but available names, descriptions and references vary. It doesn't translate missing text. A failed lookup remains visible, and a same-name result isn't substituted for the requested identifier. Different wording can be ordinary localisation; missing wording can be limited coverage. Neither observation alone establishes a separate entity.

On your own multilingual website, I would keep the person's or organisation's identifier consistent across translations and give the translated pages their own page identifiers. Then describe the relevant names and language of each page. A translated biography is another document about someone; translating it doesn't create another person.

A record isn't everything you see in Search

The Knowledge Panel is a presentation in Google Search. The underlying entity data and the API response are different layers. Google's description of Knowledge Panels says they are generated automatically from multiple sources, sometimes incorporating data partners and feedback from verified representatives. Their images can also come from different sources.

That helps explain an otherwise frustrating result: a person can have a detailed panel in Search while the API returns a sparse record. A missing image here doesn't establish that Google has no image of the person. A missing Wikipedia reference doesn't establish that Wikipedia has no article. The response gives us the fields available through this request.

Bill Slawski, patents, and the questions behind a panel

SEO by the Sea is one of my favourite places to read about how search might work. Bill Slawski's writing makes room for the mechanisms and questions behind the interface, which is considerably more interesting to me than another confident checklist promising a panel.

Bill passed away in May 2022. His writing remains a reference I value, particularly for the attention he gave to the questions behind search systems. There's still plenty to learn from following those questions, even as the systems change.

His article How is a Knowledge Panel for an Entity Triggered? examines Google's Triggering knowledge panels patent. The described process considers entities associated with a query, their topical relationship to returned search results, and whether a panel should be displayed given its content and the search results. It also describes combining content from different resources.

The useful distinction is between recognising a possible subject and deciding what to show for a particular search. Finding an entity record doesn't settle the presentation decision. The patent discusses possible conditions involving available content and search-result characteristics; it doesn't give us a public panel-eligibility calculator.

A patent documents an invention, not a complete description of today's deployed ranking and display systems. I find these documents useful for understanding problems a search engine has considered and mechanisms it has described. Turning every detail into a current SEO instruction would require evidence we don't have.

Slawski's Finding Entity Names in Google's Knowledge Graph offers another relevant thread. The underlying synonymous-name patent describes deriving candidate entity names from anchor text linking to source documents. It is a useful historical example of connecting an entity with several names and the sources describing it. Simply counting how often a name appears misses that question of reference.

Images and links in this explorer

The tool first tries an image supplied by Google. If none is usable, it can use a Wikipedia article URL supplied on that same record to retrieve a lead image with suitable attribution and licensing metadata. It doesn't search Wikipedia by name and decide that the first photograph looks close enough. Attribution, when available, appears in the inspector.

The Google Search link uses the Google MID when one is available; otherwise it searches by name. A Cloud MID isn't inserted into Google's kgmid parameter. The link opens another place to investigate, without promising a particular panel will appear.

Making your own identity clearer

For a website owner, the practical starting point is information you can maintain. Make it clear who the website represents, who wrote a piece, and which organisation or product is being described. Give people useful context in the visible page, then express the relevant details in structured data.

If I want Alexander Kyriakou on this website to be understood as the same person on my official profiles, I can publish consistent information and link those references deliberately. That is a claim about identity which other systems can evaluate. I don't control whether they accept it.

JSON-LD gives the relationships a readable structure

JSON-LD is a JSON-based format for linked data. Schema.org provides a vocabulary for describing things and their properties. Google supports JSON-LD and generally recommends it for structured data where possible. The format, the vocabulary and Google's supported search features are related, but each answers a different question.

Here is a deliberately reduced example using identifiers already present on my About page. It separates the profile page from the person it describes:

<script type="application/ld+json">
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@graph": [
    {
      "@type": "ProfilePage",
      "@id": "https://alexanderkyriakou.eu/about#profilepage",
      "url": "https://alexanderkyriakou.eu/about",
      "name": "About Alexander Kyriakou",
      "inLanguage": "en",
      "mainEntity": {
        "@id": "https://alexanderkyriakou.eu/#person"
      }
    },
    {
      "@type": "Person",
      "@id": "https://alexanderkyriakou.eu/#person",
      "name": "Alexander Kyriakou",
      "alternateName": "Alex Kyriakou",
      "url": "https://alexanderkyriakou.eu/",
      "sameAs": [
        "https://github.com/alexanderkyriakou",
        "https://www.linkedin.com/in/alexanderkyriakou"
      ]
    }
  ]
}
</script>

The profile's mainEntity refers to the person node. The person has a name, an alternative name, a website and links identifying the same person elsewhere. Google's ProfilePage guidance explains its requirements for that particular search feature; this shortened example is here to explain the relationships.

https://alexanderkyriakou.eu/#person is an identifier published on my domain. It isn't a Google MID, and adding it doesn't register me in Google's graph. The fragment distinguishes the person from the website. Reusing it allows other statements in my markup to refer to that same node instead of repeatedly introducing an unidentified person.

The @graph groups the node descriptions; the properties referring to them express the connections. Putting unrelated objects into the same array doesn't, by itself, tell a reader or machine what relationship you intended.

Be quite literal about sameAs

sameAs points to a reference page that unambiguously identifies the item. Use it for a verified identity match. An employer, a favourite publication, a loosely related Wikipedia article or another person with the same name doesn't belong there merely because the URL looks authoritative.

For an article's subject, consider about; for something referenced within it, mentions may describe the relationship. Use the property that says what you mean. A page discussing an organisation isn't necessarily that organisation's official page.

Before adding a reference, open it and check the identity. Keep existing identifiers consistent where they already represent the correct things. If a CMS and several plugins generate structured data, inspect their combined output for competing person or organisation records. More markup is not automatically more clarity.

Validate the syntax and vocabulary with the Schema Markup Validator, and use Google's Rich Results Test for its supported search features. Validation cannot establish that a biography is true or a linked profile belongs to the right person. Google's structured-data guidelines also require the markup to represent the page accurately. A green result is useful; it doesn't finish the job.

A case study worth inspecting, with the evidence kept in view

Schemantra's entity-based SEO case study describes work on a real-estate website, concentrating on choosing types and expressing relationships between listings and other entities. It reports roughly doubled organic traffic and leads, alongside increased impressions.

The useful prompt for your own work is to examine what each page represents and whether its markup communicates the relationships accurately. The reported growth is an observation from the publisher's case study. The published account doesn't isolate the contribution of the markup from other possible influences, so I wouldn't turn it into an expected uplift.

I would also examine the modelling rather than copy it wholesale. A physical apartment, the listing page advertising it and an offer to sell it are distinguishable things. Choosing how to represent them deserves more thought than finding the longest possible list of types to attach to one object.

Entities, SEO, and the arrival of more acronyms

For SEO, the practical interest is fairly direct: search systems need to interpret queries and content, and ambiguity can get in the way. The explorer gives you a way to investigate some of the returned records and their references. It can't measure how strongly a search engine associates your website with an entity, or how that association affects a ranking.

Now we have AEO, answer engine optimisation, and GEO, generative engine optimisation, alongside SEO. The labels reflect real changes in how answers are presented, although the boundaries of the services sold under them can be rather flexible. I still want the same useful question answered: what did you change, what did you observe, and what evidence connects the two?

How knowledge graphs and LLMs can work together

A language model can generate an answer about an entity without performing a live lookup in Google's Knowledge Graph. Conversely, an application can retrieve structured records and give them to a model as context. Knowing that a product uses an LLM doesn't tell us which databases, search services or retrieval methods it uses for a particular answer.

A concrete example is Microsoft's GraphRAG research. It describes using an LLM to extract an entity graph from source documents, build summaries of related groups, and use those summaries to answer questions about the collection. The graph is constructed from the supplied material. It isn't a requirement to fetch Google MIDs before those documents can be understood.

The connection also runs in the other direction: models can help decide which entity a mention refers to. Entity linking connects a mention in context to a knowledge-base entry; entity alignment concerns matching corresponding entities across knowledge graphs. Both involve more than finding similar strings.

Retrieval gives an application additional evidence to work with. It doesn't make the evidence correct by definition. A mistaken identity link can supply facts about the wrong person and still produce a very fluent answer. The interesting engineering questions include candidate selection, source quality, the ability to leave a match unresolved, and how an answer can be traced back to its supporting material.

Recent research worth following

For this subject, searching only for “MID” misses much of the relevant research. Entity linking, multilingual disambiguation, entity alignment and graph-based retrieval are more useful terms. A few papers illustrate the work:

  • ARTER: Adaptive Routing and Targeted Entity Reasoning (2025) combines candidate generation and scoring with selective LLM reasoning. It investigates when the more expensive reasoning step is useful, rather than applying it indiscriminately to every mention.
  • It's All About the Confidence (EACL 2026) studies multilingual historical entity linking. Its approach retrieves candidates, routes harder cases to an LLM and includes the possibility that no matching entry is available. Old spelling and limited context make useful tests of how easily names can mislead.
  • MERLIN (TACL 2026) provides a multilingual, multimodal entity-linking testbed using news titles and images linked to Wikidata entities. It investigates the contribution of visual context when text alone is ambiguous.

These papers investigate particular systems and benchmarks. They don't establish how Google ranks this website or how every assistant selects citations. What they do show is that identifying the right subject remains an active technical problem, even when a language model is involved.

What I would actually do with this

Start with a specific uncertainty: two records with the same name, an outdated source reference, a missing association, or different information across languages. Record what you find, inspect the sources, and correct what you control. Keep the visible information and structured data consistent. Revisit the same identifiers later if you want to observe changes.

Google's guidance for AI Overviews and AI Mode says existing SEO fundamentals remain relevant and no special Schema.org markup is required for those features. It also describes the possibility of issuing several related searches while developing a response. That is more involved than checking whether your business has a MID.

Evaluate outcomes at the level you can observe. An updated record, a changed Knowledge Panel, a citation in a generated answer and a useful visit to your website are different observations. Record the query, language, date and relevant conditions, and avoid treating one favourable answer as evidence of a general improvement.

Sometimes the uncertainty is more basic: can a crawler access the page, and has it actually visited? My robots.txt checker helps examine the rules you've published, while the Crawler IP Verifier helps check whether requests in your logs came from the crawlers they claim to be. A verified visit still doesn't tell you how the page was interpreted or whether it contributed to an answer, but it gives you one less thing to guess about.

A few practical questions

Why can't I find myself or my organisation?

Try an alternative name, remove the type filter and change the requested language. If you already have a Google or Cloud MID, try a direct lookup. An empty response tells you that this request returned no records. It isn't a verdict on your existence, your website's indexing, or every other Google system. Quite a lot to ask of an empty array.

Can I create, claim, edit or merge an entity here?

No. This explorer reads records and helps you investigate them. For a Knowledge Panel, Google provides feedback and verification routes. A claim to represent an entity is different from creating a new record, and this page doesn't perform either action.

Do I need a Wikipedia article or a Wikidata item?

Neither is a prerequisite for describing yourself accurately on your own website. If you contribute to Wikidata, follow its inclusion criteria and documentation practices. A database item should have a legitimate reason to exist within that project. Creating one solely to complete an SEO checklist is a poor starting point.

Does the copied link preserve my research?

It preserves the search settings and, when present, the inspected identifier. Opening an entity link performs a new lookup; it doesn't restore the original result list, comparison selections or a historical snapshot. The source data may have changed. Comparison selections and in-memory results disappear when the page reloads.

What leaves my browser, and what can I export?

Searches and lookups use server-side routes to request source data; this isn't an offline tool. The optional Wikidata and Wikipedia retrievals use separate routes. You don't need an account or your own API key. CSV comparison exports omit images and detailed descriptions. Review the applicable source terms before reusing the exported information.

Resources worth browsing around

The documentation and research links above sit beside the subjects they support. For broader orientation, Wikipedia's separate articles on knowledge graphs and Google's Knowledge Graph are useful starting points. Keeping those two subjects separate already clears up some of the terminology.

For a longer afternoon, follow the references through Bill Slawski's writing and into the original patents and papers. His article on personalised knowledge graphs opens another question: how useful entities can be represented even when they aren't globally prominent. That subject deserves more room than a paragraph at the end of this tool.

I started with the relatively small ambition of making entity records easier to look through. Somewhere along the way, that became a much larger question about how our names, work and relationships get represented by systems we only partly get to inspect. Fairly typical outcome for a small web project.

Anyway. Pick a name and see where it takes you.
