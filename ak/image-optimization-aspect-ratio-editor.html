Crop, optimize and publish.

I like well-optimized images online, and Squoosh is my go-to tool. But I usually had another program open just to crop an image before optimizing it. Getting a specific aspect ratio through an online cropper or a heavier image editor felt like more work than it needed to be. I'd been thinking about building on Squoosh's open-source code for a while. Here we are.

This free tool lets you crop, resize and optimize an image in one place. Keep the whole image, or choose a crop and resize and optimize that selected part directly. Useful for SEOs, copywriters, social media managers and anyone preparing images to publish.

Your images are not uploaded. Processing happens locally in your browser. The page and its tools still need to load, but your selected image stays on your device.

From image to download

  1. Choose an image. Drop a JPEG, PNG, WebP or AVIF into the tool, or select an example.
  2. Choose the frame and dimensions. Keep the original framing or select an aspect ratio. Move and resize the crop frame, then set Output width to resize the selected crop. Height follows its ratio; the tool won't upscale.
  3. Optimize, compare, download. Choose a format, quality and effort, then run Optimize. Move the comparison handle to inspect the result before downloading.

Only need the crop? Use Download crop to export it in its source format. That export uses separate encoding settings, rather than the optimization panel's settings.

Which image format should you choose?

I've kept the selection to the four formats I mainly use. They cover quite a bit of everyday web publishing.

WebP · a practical starting point

Useful for website images, including those needing transparency. My defaults are quality 80 and Balanced effort. Inspect text, edges and fine textures before deciding they suit your image.

AVIF · worth a comparison

Can produce smaller files at comparable visual quality, although results vary and encoding can take longer. It also supports transparency. Try it alongside WebP and judge both the image and the bytes.

JPEG · familiar and widely accepted

Useful for photographs and destinations expecting JPEG. Optimization uses MozJPEG, an encoder that works harder to reduce file size while retaining useful visual quality. The result is a standard JPEG. Transparency becomes a chosen background colour.

PNG · sharp detail and transparency

Useful for screenshots and graphics where lossless output matters. PNG preserves transparency. This tool provides lossless PNG export, but doesn't include a dedicated PNG optimizer.

All four work in current major browsers. Older browsers and individual publishing platforms may differ, so check the destination. WebP and AVIF exports here use lossy compression; a quality setting of 100 doesn't make them lossless. See MDN's format guide for compatibility details.

Preparing images for websites, search and social

Images on the page, page speed and Core Web Vitals

Hero images, article illustrations and product photos should keep enough detail for their displayed size without making visitors download unnecessary pixels. Smaller files can help Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) when an image is the largest visible element. Loading priority and rendering still matter. On the published page, reserve image space with dimensions to help prevent layout shifts (CLS).

SERP thumbnails and Open Graph previews

A clear subject helps an image remain useful as a small thumbnail beside a search result. Use relevant images on the page, descriptive filenames, useful alt text and nearby context. Google chooses thumbnails automatically; image metadata, including og:image and appropriate structured data, can influence its choice without guaranteeing it.

Open Graph images also represent pages when links are shared. Prepare a crop for the intended placement, then set its URL in the page's metadata. The platform presets are framing shortcuts; check the destination's dimensions and accepted formats separately.

Google Discover

Google recommends relevant, high-quality images at least 1200 pixels wide, with more than 300,000 pixels and a 16:9 ratio. A 1280 x 720 crop is a practical option if the source is large enough. Keep the important details in frame and enable large previews with max-image-preview:large. This prepares the image; it doesn't guarantee a Discover appearance.

Images in the agentic web

Some AI agents already navigate through screenshots, so visual information can matter to software using a page as well as to people reading it. As this develops, clear images, readable details and supporting text may become even more useful. Keep meaningful information intact when cropping or compressing; there's no established image setting that guarantees an agent will understand or recommend your page.

Some practical questions

Can I optimize without cropping?

Yes. Keep the original framing and run Optimize. Opening the aspect ratio panel alone doesn't crop anything; choosing a ratio starts the crop.

Can I resize just the crop?

Yes. Resize the frame to choose how much of the scene to include, then use Output width to set the crop's exported resolution. For example, a sufficiently large 16:9 crop can be reduced to 1280 x 720. Aspect ratio is its shape, dimensions are its resolution, and preview zoom is how closely you inspect it.

What do quality and effort change?

Quality controls the lossy compression trade-off. Effort controls how much processing the encoder spends: Fast, Balanced or Thorough. More effort takes longer and may save bytes, but doesn't guarantee a smaller file. Quality numbers aren't equivalent across formats. PNG uses neither control.

How are the savings calculated?

With unchanged framing and dimensions, the reference is your original file. After cropping or resizing, it's a newly encoded source-format version at the same frame and dimensions. That comparison separates optimization savings from the pixels you removed.

Why is the result larger or softer?

An already compressed image may not shrink further. Try another format, lower quality or smaller output dimensions. If detail suffers, increase quality or retain more pixels. Source-format downloads are also re-encoded, so keeping the extension doesn't preserve the original bytes.

Why won't my image open?

Use JPEG, PNG, WebP or AVIF that your browser can decode. Inputs must be at least 100 pixels on each edge, at most 32 MiB and 40 million pixels, with no edge over 16,384 pixels. HEIC, SVG, GIF and RAW aren't supported.

Why is processing slow, or the export smaller than requested?

Large images, AVIF and Thorough effort can take longer, especially on mobile. Try Balanced or Fast, or reduce Output width. Exports are limited to 16 million pixels and 8,192 pixels per edge, never intentionally upscale, and may round dimensions down to preserve the ratio.

What happens to my original, metadata and animation?

Downloading saves a new file. The original isn't overwritten, and reloading clears the editor. Exports don't retain source EXIF/GPS metadata or animation; animated inputs produce a single decoded frame. This isn't a workflow for preserving print colour profiles or HDR.
